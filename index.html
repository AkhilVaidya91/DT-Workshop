    Stage 1 · Audience perspective

    Who is it really for?

    List at least three groups of people who will use, attend, read, buy, run or be affected by what you are making. Be specific: “a commuting first-year student who works 15 hours a week” is better than “students”.

    Think first
    • Who experiences it directly (uses, attends, reads, views)? Who sets it up, pays for it, reviews it or supports it?
    • Who is affected even if they never touch it (neighbours, staff, parents, reviewers, funders)?
    • How are these groups different in goals, time, skill or access?

    Stage 5 · Interaction diagram
    
        
    Draw how people engage with your project
    
        
    Draw your interaction diagram on paper, take a photo, and give it to the LLM with the prompt below. The LLM turns your drawing into clear, end-to-end interaction flows. This works for an app, a device, an event, an exhibition or a paper's review process.
    
        
    
      
    

      
    
        
    Learn What is an interaction diagram?
    
        
    
          
    An interaction diagram (often drawn as a use-case diagram) shows who engages with your project and what they can do with it. It is a map of interactions, not a step-by-step flowchart. It has four parts:
    
          
      
            
    • Actors are stick figures. Each one is an audience group from Stage 1 (a user, an attendee, a reader, a reviewer, a vendor) or an outside system or organisation, such as a campus login, a ticketing service, a venue or a journal's submission system. Put actors who start actions on the left. Put supporting actors and outside systems on the right.
      • 
            
    • The system boundary is a large box labelled with your project's name. Everything inside the box is what your project does or offers. Everything outside is not part of it.
      • 
            
    • Use cases are ovals inside the box. Each oval is one goal an actor can reach, written as verb + noun: “Book a room”, “Buy a ticket”, “Submit a review”.
      • 
            
    • Lines connect each actor to every oval they take part in.
      • 
          
    
          
    How to draw yours: (1) Draw one stick figure per audience group. (2) For each one, write 3–6 goals as ovals. (3) Draw the system box around the ovals. (4) Connect actors to ovals. (5) Add outside systems or organisations your project depends on.
    
          
    Check: every actor has at least one line, every oval connects to an actor, and every oval name starts with a verb.
    
        
    
        
      
    

      
    
        Think first
        
      
          
    • Did you include every audience group? If one has no ovals, should they be part of the project at all?
      • 
          
    • Which goals are shared by several groups? Which belong to only one?
      • 
          
    • Which outside services, organisations or places does your project depend on?
      •

    Stage 7 · Cost
    
        
    What will it really cost?
    
        
    First, you name the main things you will have to pay for. Then the LLM researches real prices and builds a realistic estimate and budget from your list.
    
        
    
      
    

      
    
        Think first
        
      
          
    • What do you pay for once (materials, equipment, set-up), and what keeps costing money (hosting, rent, staff, subscriptions)?
      • 
          
    • Where does the money go: materials, people's time, tools and software, venue, licences, travel, printing, publication fees?
      • 
          
    • At what point in the project does each cost come in: design, build, launch, each event, each month, publication?
      • 
        
    
      
    

      
    
        
    Step 1 · Your input Core sources of cost
    
        
    List at least 4–5. For each one, briefly say what the cost is, where the money goes, and where it comes in for your project.
